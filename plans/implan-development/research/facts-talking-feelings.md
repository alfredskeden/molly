## 1. 1177 (Sweden, Swedish)

### Development 0–6 months
URL: https://www.1177.se/barn--gravid/sa-vaxer-och-utvecklas-barn/barnets-utveckling/barnets-utveckling-0-6-manader/

General: development is fastest in the first year but varies between babies, even siblings. When learning new things a baby may get upset, change mood or need more closeness. Babies communicate first through crying, then smiles, cooing and babbling.

**0–2 months (Newborn, 1 m)**
- Sounds/hearing: most babies hear well from birth; within days they can tell where a sound comes from and turn their head reflexively. Hearing is mostly fully developed by the end of the first month; they can tell speech from other sounds but don't understand meaning yet.
- Newborns seem to prefer higher voices; dislike sharp sudden sounds; are calmed by rhythmic, harmonious tones. They recognise familiar voices and prefer human voices; early on they're equally interested in all languages; prefer soft rhythmic speech. Loud sharp noise can stress them.
- They seek contact with voice, eye contact and body language. Looking at you may mean wanting contact; looking away or covering the face may mean wanting to be left alone or tiredness.
- About 2 months: pleasant cooing ("ooo"), the first step toward words.
- In the first weeks they can imitate facial movements (slowly); this fades after 1–2 months and returns about half a year later.
- Feelings: newborn smiles can be reflexive; at about 5–6 weeks a baby may smile back when you smile (early joy). Sudden sounds or something fast near the face can make them startle or cry.
- Crying: some crying without clear reason is normal; often worse in the evening; comfort and stay close. Babies cry differently for different needs.
- Try: talk about what you see and do; lean in to a few decimetres when they seek eye contact; at about 20 cm stick out your tongue, open your mouth, blink, exaggerate expressions and give them time to answer; mirror at the changing table or in front of them on their tummy; say their name, sing a short song; a parent's garment nearby when upset (but no loose fabric or pillows when sleeping).
- Get help (BVC or vårdcentral) if the baby: cries intensely or in bursts; is upset much of the day; cries a lot and eats poorly; cries and doesn't seem well.

**2–4 months (2, 3 m)**
- Feelings: starts to sense own body (touching fingers and toes); begins to recognise things and people; remembers and expects routines (bath, nappy); shows emotions more clearly (smiles at family, angry or upset e.g. when put to bed).
- Sounds: the newborn head-turn reflex fades; at about 3 months turns head on purpose toward a sound such as a parent's voice. May not smile until they see who is speaking or singing.
- Wants to be part of things, face to face, in the same room. Communicates with eye contact, touch, body language, expressions, crying and sounds. Vowel sounds, trying sounds, imitating some sounds, smiling or babbling back; babbling can turn into crying.
- Intense contact tires them quickly: signs are sudden crying, yawning, turning the head away, closing the eyes.
- End of this period: many parents hear the first laugh.
- Children growing up with more than one language develop language the same way; learning two is not necessarily harder.
- Try: repeat routines (a short rhyme at nappy changes); play with words, describe what you did/do/will do, say how you and they feel; smile and sound cheerful; be exaggeratedly clear with expressions, gestures, repetition.
- No specific warning signs listed for this band.

**4–6 months (4, 5 m)**
- Many react to their name. What matters is that you talk and answer when the baby tries to communicate; babbling gets more varied.
- Common: consonant + vowel babble ("ba", "ga", "dä"); simple peekaboo and give-and-take games; hears your tone (angry vs happy); starts reacting to some words like "no"; tests voice volume; may follow your gaze; many laugh out loud for the first time.
- Feelings: shows more clearly whom they want to be close to; may stop smiling at everyone. Understands they can influence others ("if I shout, Dad comes"); calmed by seeing or hearing a parent at a distance; expects a smile or sound back; matches their feelings to yours; plays and "talks" for longer.
- Give attention and show extra joy when they're happy. Needs structure: play and rest.
- A parent's own wellbeing affects how they can communicate with the baby (links to postnatal depression).
- Try: stories, rhymes, songs, books; describe what they see; toys with different sounds; rhyme games with touch (e.g. foot rhymes); a first photo book of family, toys, pets.
- No specific warning signs listed. General: contact BVC if you need support, have questions or are worried; also vårdcentral or the municipality (parent support).

### Development 6–12 months
URL: https://www.1177.se/barn--gravid/sa-vaxer-och-utvecklas-barn/barnets-utveckling/barnets-utveckling-6-12-manader/

**6–8 months (6, 7 m)**
- Many babble with consonants ("ma-ma-ma", "ba-ba-ba"), imitate the language around them, play with sounds; listen closely and react to their name.
- Begin to understand some words like "no" (but can't be expected to stop when told). Tell questions from statements. Use their voice to show happiness or anger. Can signal hunger, sleepiness or play, and may call out instead of screaming.
- Notice other babies: smile at them, crawl toward them, try to take toys; curious but don't play together yet.
- Feelings: many are a bit shy or worried about the unfamiliar. In the second half of the first year curiosity often dips for a while; more dependent, seek closeness. Stranger anxiety is common (may cry when unknown people come close, or be shy even with people met often); usually temporary. It shows the baby is learning you're their most important source of safety. May protest separation and be harder to comfort for less familiar people. Let them meet people and situations at their own pace. Some sleep less soundly (routines help); some find comfort in a soft toy or blanket; can be clingy while exploring.
- Try: answer their sounds; read, point and look at books (chewable board books), following their gaze; roll a ball and name the turns; describe sights and sounds on walks; songs, rhymes, finger games; library.

**8–10 months (8, 9 m)**
- Many understand words before speaking (turn toward a sibling or teddy when named). Like picture books with large familiar objects.
- End of this period: a few say first real words ("titta" = look); for most it takes longer. Babble can be hard to tell from words: answer with the real word. Show joy when first words come.
- Common: favourite sounds repeated, understanding many words, recognising songs, turn-taking; more persistent; shows frustration by throwing things or grimacing; may shake head for no.
- Feelings: may worry when you leave and follow you out of sight; separation anxiety and fear of strangers lessen over time. Fear of strangers is part of development and helps them explore at a suitable pace. Toward the end many become more outgoing again. Expect routines; prefer certain people and toys; may sleep less soundly and dream more.
- Try: point at pictures, name things and their sounds; name what they look at; tell what comes next in the day; clapping and finger games, peekaboo; repeat what they say, encourage imitating sounds.

**10–12 months (10, 11 m)**
- Follow someone's pointing gaze; start gesturing (showing, pointing). Some point at 8 months, others not until 16 months. After 1 year they often point and vocalise together.
- Most say first real words around 1 year, but it varies widely: some at 10 months, others at 17 months or later. Long babble sequences that sound like speech. Understand many common words.
- Common: point at objects like a lamp or clock; try to copy words; follow simple requests (fetch, pat, hug, kiss, wave); say no and know what it means (often head-shake); wave bye-bye. Photos understood before drawings; short attention span.
- Feelings: share others' feelings (happy when someone is happy); take cues from adults: if they fall unhurt and you look worried they may cry, and your calm face can calm them. Expect someone to come when they call. Fear of strangers often lessens. Want to be where parents are and copy them. Own will clearer: want to do it themselves, protest help, feel pride.
- Try: respond to gestures as if they meant a bit more; name what happens around them; let them help (fetching); songs with animal sounds; rhymes where they fill in a word or gesture; peekaboo.
- Whole page: no specific warning signs; seek help (BVC, vårdcentral, municipality) if worried. Don't compare with other babies.

### Development 1–2 years (12, 15, 18, 21 m pages)
URL: https://www.1177.se/barn--gravid/sa-vaxer-och-utvecklas-barn/barnets-utveckling/barnets-utveckling-1-2-ar/

Talking
- Understand much more than they can say; by the end of this year they understand most of everyday talk. Much communication is gestures and pointing (wanting, showing, asking, exploring); later combined with sounds and eye contact. May get frustrated when not understood.
- Many say first words around 1, timing varies a lot. First words are names of important things ("mamma", "pappa", "vovve", "sko"). Around 1 a single word can mean a whole sentence ("Pappa" = "Daddy, I want food"). Early words can mean something different (e.g. "doggie" for all four-legged animals). Many have their own words.
- Many have a fast vocabulary growth from about 1.5 to 2 years. Around 2, many have between 50 and several hundred words (varies widely).
- Toward the end of the second year many make two-word sentences ("ha den", "mamma bil"), most common around 2. Use words for needs and feelings; say "mine" toward the end.
- Common: fetch an item when asked; point to body parts when asked ("Where's your nose?").
- "No": many discover and understand "no" around 1 and try it out; first step toward own will.
- Girls talk somewhat earlier on average, but differences between individual children are bigger than between girls and boys.
- Multilingual children develop language at the same age as monolingual; best at the language they use most (can change); mixing languages for periods is common.
- Screens: Folkhälsomyndigheten guideline: no screens at all under 2. Children learn best with people; screens make them passive. If used: age-suited, watch together, talk about it; no news or violent films in the background; adults' own screen use matters.
- Try: short-text books, point and talk; repeat songs, rhymes, finger games; talk a lot (name what a quiet child does without expecting an answer); listen patiently; repeat and expand ("Melwin ner" → "Yes, you went down all by yourself"); use their words and add new ones; **don't correct mistakes**; follow their pointing.

Feelings and social
- Start to see themselves as their own person: pride and shame. Recognise themselves in mirrors and photos. Understand right and wrong but can't control impulses yet. Mood swings fast. Can't handle strong feelings alone; need adult help.
- May worry in new developmental phases and want more closeness. **Around 1.5 years** more sensitive to separations again; may stop enjoying something they liked; may worry about being left.
- Close adults are the secure base for exploring. Do roughly the same thing at every drop-off and pick-up (e.g. preschool).
- Look to adults' reactions to interpret the world; start to sense others have feelings; a one-year-old may try to comfort a crying child the way they were comforted.
- More own will, **especially around 1.5 years**: running off, refusing the pram. This is normal; they need to practise strong feelings and see how adults react. They need to feel loved even when they don't do what you want.
- (1177 does not use the word "tantrum" on this page.) Explain briefly why not; distract; swap a taken object for another; offer two choices; name feelings ("You're angry because the tower fell…"); show how to handle frustration; stay close and comfort when disappointed.
- Play: like being near other children, sometimes play together but more often side by side; need adult help with turns. Pretend play grows when adults join in.
- Preschool start often around 1.5 years: settling-in helps; some sleep more restlessly and need more closeness at first; usually goes well quickly.
- No specific warning signs listed; contact BVC, preschool staff, vårdcentral or municipality if worried.

### Development 2–3 years (24, 30, 36 m pages)
URL: https://www.1177.se/barn--gravid/sa-vaxer-och-utvecklas-barn/barnets-utveckling/barnets-utveckling-2-3-ar/

Talking
- Most children talk at 2. Language grows fast: first two-word sentences, then up to about seven words as they near 3. Typical 2–3: sentences of three to six words, correct word order, pronouns (I/me, you, we, they).
- Understand two- or three-part instructions (go to the room, fetch the teddy and the fire truck); understand on/in/under/over. The family usually understands most of what the child says.
- About 2: short conversations if you keep them going. "What's that?" is common. May invent words.
- Swapping sounds and mispronunciations are common (two consonants together are hard, e.g. "tätta" for "tvätta"); usually sorts itself out, not a worry at this age. Don't correct; say the word yourself.
- Page does not mention hearing or stuttering.
- Screens: Folkhälsomyndigheten: ages 2–5 at most one hour a day; watch together, no violent adult films or news, turn off background noise.
- Try: read every evening if you can; repeat and expand; sound games (vacuum, aeroplane, dripping water); talk about shared experiences and when the child was little; outings and talk afterwards; songs and rhymes; open questions about books.

Feelings and social
- Start to understand they and parents have different needs, but their own come first; hard to combine wants. Moods change fast (hugs to outbursts); can't handle strong feelings alone.
- Want to do more themselves (eat, dress, doors); often want to do more than they can.
- "Mine" and "no" are frequent words; sharing is hard; lend toys then want them back.
- Tantrums/defiance: often want things they can't have; strong reactions, outbursts; some react faster, harder and more often than others. Need patience, kindness, warmth; a harsh style isn't good long term. Parents getting angry sometimes is OK within reason; setting a limit is different from losing your temper. If too angry, step away and let another adult take over. Repair after conflicts quickly. Being a steady caring adult matters more than perfect consistency.
- Avoid conflicts: distract, simple choices, name and acknowledge feelings, prepare for what's next, describe what's happening, sometimes let them have their way when it matters little.
- Temperament: shy/cautious children need a calm, encouraging, waiting parent; active/impulsive children need a more active, clear style, planning ahead and outlets for energy.
- Peers matter more; less dependent on parental closeness; many start playing with others themselves, easier as language develops. Pretend play with sequences.
- Still need closeness; some sleep restlessly and may need to sleep with a parent for a while. Many also like playing alone.
- No specific warning signs; contact BVC, preschool, vårdcentral or municipality if worried. Prematurity, disability or big life changes can affect development.

### Crying ("När små barn skriker")
URL: https://www.1177.se/barn--gravid/att-skota-ett-nyfott-barn/nar-sma-barn-skriker/
- Why: closeness, hunger/thirst, boredom, needing calm and rest, pain (often tummy, common in the first months), discomfort (nappy, position), too hot/cold. Early on crying mostly means wanting closeness, food or company. Irregular day–night rhythm means night crying too.
- How much: varies a lot between babies. Up to about 3–4 months many have a period of harder-to-soothe crying, usually late afternoon/evening; it passes. (No peak week given.)
- Soothe: hold close, carrier or wrap; if overstimulated lay them down safely and step back a little; some calm to vacuum/dishwasher/music/traffic sounds; feed often in small amounts; walk outside or roll the pram over a threshold; looser clothes; gentle massage (some dislike it); for tummy pain belly massage or knees to tummy; something cold to suck if teething; dim light and quiet for an overtired baby, soft singing, rhythmic rocking, patting. Try methods but don't switch too fast. Share the soothing.
- **Never shout back, grab or shake the baby.** Shaking or rough handling is life-threatening; under 1 year it can cause very serious lasting injury, and even a few seconds can harm. Get support if very frustrated or angry.
- Coping: rest when the baby sleeps, eat, ask for help with chores; if no one else is there, put the baby down safely for a few minutes and go to another room to breathe. Talk to the BVC nurse (can refer to psychologist or doctor). Feeling angry, scared, guilty or inadequate is common; arguing about crying doesn't mean the relationship is in trouble.
- Contact BVC if: worried about how much the baby cries; baby is 2 weeks–4 months and you think it may be colic; you need support to cope.
- Contact vårdcentral/open clinic promptly if: intense or bursts of crying that can't be soothed; crying or whimpering most of the day; crying differently (whimpering, moaning); not acting like themselves. Emergency department if closed. Call 1177 for advice.
- Possible medical causes: ear pain, fever, tummy pain, teething, colic.

### Colic ("Spädbarnskolik")
URL: https://www.1177.se/barn--gravid/vanliga-besvar-och-sjukdomar-hos-barn/vanliga-sjukdomar-och-besvar-hos-barn-0-1-ar/spadbarnskolik/
- Definition: a small baby screams intensely for several hours, at least three times a week, usually at about the same time of day. Also called "three-month colic".
- Timing: most often between **2 weeks and 5 months**, often worst at **6–8 weeks**; common; usually goes within a few months.
- Signs: hours of intense screaming (one stretch or several), same time daily, mostly afternoon/evening; red face from straining; knees drawn up, stiff legs, rumbling tummy, trouble passing stool.
- Cause unclear; swallowed air while eager sucking may play a role; both breast- and formula-fed. Sometimes the baby needs more food; BVC can weigh and measure.
- Try: pauses to burp during feeds; smaller teat holes / smaller amounts of formula; good latch, express a little first, feed often in small amounts; burp upright over the shoulder; closeness, massage, sling; dummy if strong need to suck; if breastfeeding stop smoking/snus; avoid caffeine; rectal gas tube (pysventil) when calm; bath or massage just before the usual crying time. Some parents find colic drops (non-prescription) helpful.
- Don't cut foods (yours or the baby's) without talking to BVC or a doctor first; a doctor examines before e.g. cow's milk elimination. Breastfed babies usually don't have cow's milk protein problems.
- **Never shake**: life-threatening, even a few seconds can cause permanent injury. If you feel you might: put the baby safely in the cot (even crying; another adult can stay), leave the room, calm down, come back when in control.
- Contact BVC if: lots of crying many days in a row; you're exhausted; you want support; baby unhappy much of the day; formula-fed and you suspect cow's milk/food allergy; feeding problems (BVC or breastfeeding clinic).
- Emergency department immediately if: crying in bursts and can't be comforted (especially if rotavirus vaccine in the past week); not acting normally (limp, refuses food, cries differently, moans, whimpers). 1177 by phone 24/7.
- Parents: colic isn't caused by anything you did; it passes; ask relatives, take turns sleeping; BVC can connect you with a doctor or psychologist.

## 2. Rikshandboken barnhälsovård (Sweden, Swedish, for professionals)

### What BVC checks, and referral criteria (language exam and screening)
URL: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/kommunikativ-utveckling/sprakundersokning-och-sprakscreening/
- Language/communication assessed at 0–3 m, 3–5 m, 6–8 m, 10 m, 12 m, 1.5 y, (4 y, 5 y). Formal language screening at **2.5 or 3 years** (regional; some regions only at 3). Parents' concerns are always noted and explored.
- Referral when a child doesn't develop as expected in communication, babbling, language or speech, or has feeding/swallowing difficulties. Frequent drooling after 3 → doctor.
- **0–3 m (6–8 weeks):** baby should answer with a smile and contented sounds when spoken to and make eye contact. If not: check vision, hearing, parent–baby interaction, parental depression.
- **3–5 m:** varied vowel sounds; smiling and vocal answers followed up.
- **6 m:** many babble different vowels in sequence, gliding between them; some consonant–vowel. (No referral criteria at 6 m on this page.)
- **8 m:** babble more varied (consonants + vowels); interest in talking, eye contact, contact with parent. Follow up if no babbling or no interest in conversation. **Refer for hearing assessment if the baby doesn't babble, isn't interested in voices, or doesn't react to sounds.**
- **10 m:** shared focus with an adult; requests by pointing or reaching and looking at the partner; understands several words; enjoys peekaboo; imitates (blocks knocked together); "Where's the lamp?" comprehension. Babbling should be well developed: varied syllable babble ("ba", "ba-ba-ba", "nana"). Extra follow-up / possible referral (speech therapist, psychologist, doctor) if the child doesn't imitate, doesn't look for a hidden ball, doesn't follow instructions, doesn't enjoy peekaboo, or has no syllable babble. A deaf or hard-of-hearing child doesn't develop advanced babbling; babbling stops. Late babbling is linked to later language/speech delay.
- **12 m:** consider referral if no varied consonant–vowel babble, or not communicating with gestures and eye contact.
- **18 m (17–19 m):** first full language assessment. About **8–10 words** or expressions used often and to communicate (animal/vehicle sounds like "woof" count; mere repetition doesn't); multilingual children: add words in all languages. Understands far more than says; most understand well over 10 words; points to body parts; fetches an object on request. Shared focus established; pretend play (pretend play and understanding predict later language better than word count). Follow up in 2–3 months or refer (speech therapist, hearing assessment, psychologist) if: not about 8–10 words; understands fewer than about 10 words; doesn't follow simple requests; no pretend play.
- **2.5 / 3 years screening:** most important is finding **comprehension** problems (parents rarely notice, because children use context and parents adapt their speech). Language difficulties are often an early sign of other conditions (autism, attention/behaviour); in Swedish studies about 60% of children referred for language at 2.5 had neurodevelopmental diagnoses at 6–7. Two methods: Västra Götaland toy-based screening (2 y 4 m – 2 y 8 m) and the Uppsala model with photos (2 y 6 m – 3 y 2 m). Parents' concern must always be taken seriously. Pronunciation alone: referral only if the child is hard for the family to understand or has severe speech problems (only vowels, many words starting with a vowel or "h", most consonants made far back, e.g. "titta" → "kika"). Language disorder prevalence about 10% among preschoolers.
  - **2.5 y (Västra Götaland) refer to speech therapist if:** fewer than 25 words; no two-word sentences; doesn't understand simple requests; parents worried. Follow up at 3 if 25–50 words, parents worried, or poor participation but seems to understand.
  - **3 y (Västra Götaland) refer if:** fewer than 50 own words; no three-word sentences; doesn't understand two-step instructions; doesn't understand "in" and "on"; so hard to understand that only one parent or a sibling understands; parents worried.
  - **Uppsala 2.5 y refer if:** understands fewer than 3 of 5 standard questions; won't take part after two tries; very hard to understand even for parents; doesn't follow instructions without gestures; parents worried. No two-word sentences heard but passes the questions → redo at 3.
  - **Uppsala 3 y refer if:** fewer than 3 of 5 questions; no three-word sentences heard; won't take part; very hard to understand even for parents; needs gestures to follow instructions; parents worried.
  - Also at 3: refer if drooling, chewing/swallowing difficulty (doctor first), or stuttering.
  - Some regions combine 2.5 y screening with the M-CHAT questionnaire and a joint-attention observation; some use the Infant-Toddler Checklist at 18 m.
- Page does not cover loss of skills (regression).

### Typical communication, language and speech development
URL: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/kommunikativ-utveckling/barnets-kommunikations--sprak--och-talutveckling/
- 0–3 m: communication is instinctive; caregivers learn to tell cries apart (nappy, comfort, food); baby imitates faces, later sounds; cooing; vowel range grows to resemble the home language.
- 3–5 m: reaches for things (read as pointing); starts looking between caregiver and object (start of joint attention); intentional communication; turn-taking "conversations".
- 5–10 m: babble with the melody of surrounding speech; real pointing; gestures (arms up to be picked up, "gone", waving bye-bye). **Syllable babbling usually 6–8 months; a typically developing child babbles in syllables by 10 months regardless of background.** Babbling predicts later speech. Many words understood around 6 months (body parts, mum, dad).
- 12–18 m: many one-year-olds say a few words the family understands (often animal/vehicle sounds); mostly gestures and sounds; frequent pointing and showing; vocabulary grows monthly; fetches objects; joint attention more advanced.
- 1.5 y: some learn steadily, others have a vocabulary spurt (often 1.5–2 y). Understanding always ahead of speech. At about 50 words, two-word combinations start (first word + gesture, then two words, "daddy shoe").
- 2–3 y: big variation. By about 2 should begin to express themselves in speech. Pronunciation is most noticed by parents, but a small vocabulary and short sentences affect the child more. Longer sentences, pronouns, plurals; multi-step instructions. Speech should include a variety of consonants, not only vowels. Hesitations and repetitions common and often temporary.
- **Not combining two words at 2 raises the risk of language difficulties at school age** (93% of those who didn't had difficulties at school start, per the handbook).
- Risk factors: family history (half of children with language disorder have a parent with language difficulties), poor condition at birth / low Apgar / low birth weight, being a boy, social factors, temperament; protective: responsive caregivers who talk a lot, good preschool. Hearing impairment affects about 2% of preschool children (mentioned among associated conditions).
- Stuttering (index page): 5–10% of all children have had a period of stuttering or stutter-like speech; early intervention can matter.

### Socio-emotional development (typical)
URL: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/socioemotionell-utveckling/typisk-socioemotionell-utveckling/
- Social smile around **8 weeks**, in response to a parent's soft voice or smile.
- From the first months babies share emotions (laughter spreads; one crying upsets others).
- Second half of first year: stronger bond with parent; show who is familiar and who isn't; often react with worry or sadness when left.
- End of first year: communicate with body language, expressions, pointing, shared attention; enjoy peekaboo; respond to their name.
- Second year: share attention with an adult (looking at a picture book together). 1–2 y: pretend play (phone, feeding a doll), play alongside other children. Children more often comfort upset peers and show concern; check a parent's reaction in new situations; move further away while keeping track of the parent. Simple understanding that others have thoughts and feelings by about 2.
- 2–3 y: ability to influence own emotional state develops quickly; large individual differences (temperament, environment). Third year: testing limits and independence; sharing more common but perspective-taking still hard, so conflicts are common. By 3: want to play together, role play.

### Socio-emotional development (atypical, signs)
URL: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/socioemotionell-utveckling/avvikande-socioemotionell-utveckling/
- Autism signs in small children (no ages given): doesn't respond adequately to others' social approaches; little interest in other children; unusual reactions to sounds, tastes, smells; intense/unusual interests; repetitive movements. Occasional autism-like behaviours are common; diagnosis needs psychologist and doctor.
- Separation anxiety is common in infants and usually fades around 2–3 years; if it doesn't, or fear when a parent leaves is excessive for the age, or constant worry something will happen to a parent, the family may need help.
- Strong shyness leading to avoiding many social situations may be an early sign of social anxiety. Selective mutism (talks at home, not at preschool; or stops talking) lasting months → assessment.
- Attachment disorder signs: indiscriminate, uninhibited contact with any adult; marked emotional distance (doesn't seek or accept comfort); few positive emotions or little interest in people.
- Follow-up points for socio-emotional development: 6 and 8 weeks, 6 m, 10 m, 18 m, 2.5 and 3 y, 4 y, 5 y. (Page doesn't mention eye contact, response to name, pointing or loss of skills as signs.)

### Defiance and independence ("Trots")
URL: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/socioemotionell-utveckling/trots-och-sjalvstandighetsutveckling/
- Defiance is a normal, necessary part of becoming independent ("no", "can do it myself", "don't want to"). Often the child just follows their own idea; impulse control and sense of consequences aren't developed yet.
- Peaks: often more protests around **18 months** and **2.5–3 years**; usually settles around **4**. At 18 m: "no" arrives, transitions (bedtime, teeth) trigger protests, easier to distract. At 2.5–3: may seem to change personality (irritable, stubborn); frustration about what they can't control; may hold it together at preschool and let it out at home.
- About **9 in 10 preschool children have outbursts, about 1 in 10 daily**. Milder outbursts with an understandable cause carry less risk than unpredictable, long, destructive ones.
- Temperament matters; unresponsive handling prolongs difficult periods.
- Respond: attune to the feeling first, name it briefly; calm voice, few words, few questions; calm first, reason later; stay nearby (young children shouldn't be left alone to calm down; sit close if they don't want to be held); state the limit when needed; stop danger immediately and talk afterwards; warn before transitions; routines, picture schedules; humour and distraction; lots of positive attention; 10–15 minutes of child-led play daily.
- Avoid: lots of reprimands; mostly negative attention; "stop it" instead of "do this"; reasoning with an upset child; leaving a young child alone to calm down; expecting more self-control than possible.
- Help: start with BVC parenting support; group or individual support (BHV psychologist); BUP for more severe; report if child is at risk. Oppositional defiant disorder requires at least six months of hostile, defiant behaviour affecting daily life.

### Follow-up per visit (what BVC observes)
- 6–8 weeks: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/utvecklingsuppfoljning-i-olika-aldrar/psykomotorisk-utveckling-6-8-veckor/ — baby smiles and makes answering sounds when spoken to or smiled at. Not responding → rule out sense organ problems, interaction difficulties, parental low mood/depression.
- 6 months: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/utvecklingsuppfoljning-i-olika-aldrar/psykomotorisk-utveckling-6-manader/ — answers with eye contact or sounds when spoken to; if not, possibly hearing referral.
- 10 months: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/utvecklingsuppfoljning-i-olika-aldrar/utvecklingsuppfoljning-10-manader/ — understands single words ("Where's the lamp?"), does block-knocking together and imitates, reacts to peekaboo. Not understanding → possibly hearing referral.
- 18 months: page content not retrievable (client-rendered); see the 18 m criteria above.
- 2.5 and 3 years: https://www.rikshandboken-bhv.se/halsobesok/utvecklingsuppfoljning/utvecklingsuppfoljning-i-olika-aldrar/utvecklingsuppfoljning--25-och-3-ar/ — expected two-word phrases at 2.5 and three-word phrases at 3; simple role play with toys ("feed the doll"); points to and names doll, car, ball, dog. ## 4. NHS (International, English, OGL)

### Help your baby learn to talk
URL: https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/help-your-baby-learn-to-talk/ (reviewed 26 Apr 2023; review due 26 Apr 2026)
- 0–6 m: hold close and look at them while you talk (they watch faces); narrate feeding, changing, bath; sing (rhythm of language); repeat their sounds back (listening, turn-taking); sing-song voice.
- 6–12 m: name and point ("Look, a cat", then "a black cat"); books, just talk about the pictures; **dummy only at sleep time, aim to stop completely by 12 months** (hard to learn to talk with a dummy in); peekaboo for turn-taking, attention, listening.
- 12–18 m: if they say a word wrong, say it correctly back ("Yes, it's a cat"); don't criticise or tell them off; offer choices ("apple or banana?"); noisy toys and books; action songs and nursery rhymes.
- 18–24 m: repeat words in everyday phrases; short simple instructions ("Get your coat"); "Where's your…?" body parts; limit daily TV (stories and conversation help more).
- 2–3 y: children start simple sentences around 2; reply with sentences a few words longer; say their name first to get attention and give time to answer; teach group words ("toy"); symbolic sounds ("uh-oh", "meow"); switch off background TV and radio; talk during chores.
- Bilingual: another language can help English develop; use the language you're comfortable with; one parent per language is fine, children adapt well.
- Help: if worried, talk to your GP or health visitor, who can refer to speech and language therapy; you can also self-refer to a speech and language therapist. Speech and Language UK and Words for Life linked.
- Page does not cover hearing tests or hearing-loss signs. A video mentions children start talking roughly 1 to 2.5 years.

### Soothing a crying baby
URL: https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/soothing-a-crying-baby/ (reviewed 22 Apr 2026)
- Why: hunger, dirty/wet nappy, tiredness, wanting a cuddle, wind, too hot/cold, boredom, overstimulation.
- Hard-to-settle crying most common afternoon/evening; **crying usually increases from around 2 weeks and gradually reduces around 3 months**.
- Soothe: let a breastfed baby suckle; gentle background noise; hold close or sling (safe babywearing) and sway, talk, sing; rock the pram, walk or drive (if asleep in the car seat move to a firm flat surface soon); music, rattle, mobile; stroke the back; warm bath (some like it, some don't); too much rocking can keep them awake.
- Crying during feeds: check positioning and latch; could be reflux, speak to health visitor or GP.
- Colic possible under 5 months: waves of crying for hours; comfort and wait. Constant crying you can't console, or a cry different from normal, may mean illness: GP or NHS 111 (e.g. high temperature, not interested in feeding).
- Call 999 if: fit; blue/grey/pale/blotchy skin, lips or tongue (on dark skin check palms/soles); unresponsive, floppy or hard to wake; breathing difficulty (grunting, sucking under ribs, very fast); repeated forceful vomiting; high or low temperature; rash that doesn't fade under pressure; no wet nappy in 8 hours. Trust your instincts.
- Coping: talk to a friend, health visitor or GP; Cry-sis helpline 0800 448 0737 (9am–10pm daily); keep a crying diary; feeling overwhelmed is common; if stressed and no one can take over, put the baby somewhere safe (cot, pram), close the door, go to another room, set a time limit (e.g. 10 minutes).
- **Never shake your baby**: shaking moves the head violently and can cause brain damage.

### Colic
URL: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/colic/ (reviewed 26 Apr 2022; review due 26 Apr 2025, overdue)
- Definition: crying a lot with no obvious cause. **More than 3 hours a day, 3 days a week, for at least 1 week, in an otherwise healthy baby.** Common; can start a few weeks old, usually stops by **3 to 4 months**. Often worse afternoon/evening.
- Signs while crying: hard to settle, clenched fists, red face, knees to tummy or arched back, rumbling tummy or lots of wind.
- Other causes to consider: hunger, nappy, wind, reflux, constipation.
- Try: hold/cuddle; sit or hold upright during feeds; wind after feeds; rock over the shoulder or in basket/pram; warm bath; gentle white noise; keep feeding as usual; breastfeeding mothers don't need to change diet.
- Not recommended: anti-colic drops, herbal remedies, probiotics (no evidence); spinal manipulation or cranial osteopathy (little evidence, may hurt). A colicky baby usually doesn't need a doctor. (Note: differs from 1177, which says some parents find colic drops helpful.)
- Non-urgent (NHS 111, GP, health visitor): worried; nothing works; hard to cope; not growing or gaining weight; symptoms after 4 months.
- A&E or 999: weak or high-pitched cry; cry that doesn't sound like their normal cry. Trust instincts.
- Cause unknown; possibly food harder to digest; some cry from food allergy such as cows' milk.
- Support: other parents, family, Cry-sis 0800 448 0737. (This page does not mention shaking; the crying page does.)

### Temper tantrums
URL: https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/behaviour/temper-tantrums/ (reviewed 7 Nov 2022)
- Usually start around **18 months**, very common in toddlers; less likely once they can talk more; by **4** far less common.
- Why: want to express themselves but can't, frustration; triggers tiredness, hunger, frustration, jealousy.
- Handle: find the cause (tired, hungry?); accept their anger; distract quickly; stay calm and wait (shouting back doesn't help; ignore onlookers); **don't turn a no into a yes or bribe with treats**; at home you can go into another room once they can't hurt themselves; short shopping trips that involve them; some parents hold the child firmly (best when more upset than angry and you're calm); hitting/biting/kicking: don't hit back, say it hurts and isn't allowed, find what's worrying them, praise good behaviour; let them burn off feelings in a big space; name their feelings.
- Help: if seriously concerned, health visitor or GP; Family Lives helpline 0808 800 2222.
- (Note: NHS allows going to another room; Rikshandboken says young children shouldn't be left alone to calm down; CDC suggests ignoring and giving time. Worth wording carefully: "stay calm and nearby".)

## 5. Cross-source facts by app page (quick pick list)

- **Newborn (0–4 wk):** hears well from birth, turns toward sounds, prefers familiar voices (1177). Crying is the main signal; some unexplained crying normal, often evenings (1177). Crying rises from ~2 weeks (NHS). Never shake (1177, NHS). Get help: intense/burst crying, upset most of the day, crying + poor feeding, not well (1177).
- **1 m:** first social smiles at ~5–6 weeks (1177); social smile ~8 weeks (RHB). BVC 6–8 wk checks smile + answering sounds; if absent, check hearing/vision/interaction/parental mood (RHB). Colic window 2 wk–5 m, worst 6–8 wk (1177); >3 h/day, 3 days/week, ≥1 week (NHS).
- **2 m:** cooing ~2 m (1177); CDC 2 m list. Colic peak.
- **3 m:** turns head to a voice on purpose ~3 m (1177); first laughs toward end of 2–4 m (1177); crying eases ~3 m (NHS), harder-to-soothe period up to 3–4 m (1177).
- **4–5 m:** CDC 4 m list; consonant-vowel babble, reacts to name, laughs out loud, more choosy about who they smile at (1177 4–6 m). Colic usually gone by 3–4 m (NHS) / 5 m (1177); NHS: see GP if colic symptoms after 4 m.
- **6–7 m:** CDC 6 m list; "ma-ma-ma" babble, stranger anxiety starts, separation protests (1177 6–8 m); syllable babble usually 6–8 m (RHB).
- **8–9 m:** BVC 8 m: refer for hearing if no babble / no interest in voices / no reaction to sounds (RHB). CDC 9 m list (stranger shyness, reacts when you leave). Quick cheerful goodbye (CDC). Dummy only for sleep, stop by 12 m (NHS).
- **10–11 m:** syllable babble by 10 m in every typically developing child (RHB); pointing 8–16 m (1177); first words 10–17 m or later (1177). BVC 10 m checks.
- **12 m:** first words around 1 (1177, RHB); CDC 12 m list; BVC 12 m: refer if no varied babble or no gestures/eye contact (RHB).
- **15 m:** CDC 15 m list; tantrums expected (CDC).
- **18 m:** about 8–10 words, understands >10, follows simple request, pretend play (RHB referral thresholds); CDC 18 m list (3+ words, one-step direction without gestures); defiance/tantrums often start/peak ~18 m (RHB, NHS); separation sensitivity around 1.5 y and preschool start (1177).
- **21 m:** vocabulary spurt 1.5–2 y (1177, RHB); two-word combos start at ~50 words (RHB). No CDC checklist.
- **24 m:** 50 to several hundred words, two-word sentences most common ~2 (1177); CDC 24 m list; no two-word combos at 2 is a risk sign (RHB); empathy (CDC, RHB).
- **30 m:** language screening 2.5 y (RHB criteria: <25 words, no 2-word sentences, doesn't understand simple requests, parent worry); CDC 30 m list (~50 words); defiance second peak 2.5–3 y (RHB); 9 in 10 have outbursts, 1 in 10 daily (RHB).
- **36 m:** 3-y screening (RHB: <50 words, no 3-word sentences, not 2-step instructions, not "in/on", only understood by one parent/sibling, parent worry; drooling, chewing, stuttering); sentences of 3–6 words, family understands most (1177); CDC 36 m list (understood by others most of the time, calms within 10 min after drop-off); separation anxiety usually fades by 2–3 y (RHB); tantrums far less common by 4 (NHS, RHB).
- **Loss of skills:** only CDC states it explicitly ("has lost skills" → talk to the doctor). 1177 and RHB pages read do not mention regression.
